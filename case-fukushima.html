Fukushima Daiichi, 2011

An earthquake and tsunami produced a cascade across four reactor units and consequences still unfolding. The program uses the event to show why a failure is not the same thing as its trigger.

Public record
Source: World Nuclear Association

What the record shows

The Fukushima Daiichi plant was built about 10 metres above sea level. In 2002 its design-basis tsunami was revised to 5.7 metres. In 2008, its operator calculated a potential tsunami height of 15.7 metres at the site.

On 11 March 2011, earthquake damage cut all six external power supplies. The tsunami then drowned the diesel generators and inundated the electrical switchgear and batteries. Explosions followed at Units 1, 3 and 4 between 12 and 15 March.

Narrative reading
From the film below

The seam had been left open

Stop the story at the shock and Fukushima looks like any other earthquake. The program argues that filing is wrong. What failed was a gap between the wall and the coast that had been visible in parts for years, but that nothing was measuring as a quantity, so it could be deferred. The rupture was not the minute of the quake. It was the seam the quake found.

From there the site stopped being one event. Each unit followed its own path on its own clock, and the clocks then got longer: reactor fleets offline, energy imports, stalled supply chains, a nuclear phase-out decided in another country, decommissioning on a decades-long horizon. The shock lasted days. The loss did not.

Narrative reading

What this case is, and is not

The film is a narrative reading of the public record. It states that SCFL’s six operators were not run for it, and it reports no computed SCFL result. Single-event verification work in the nuclear-cascade domain is reported separately in the Operators Test and Validation Notebook.

The case is used to frame a question: who would read such a structure, and on which clock? An infrastructure owner reads power, cooling and the neighbouring unit. A shipper reads the lane and the bill of lading. A reinsurer reads how a closed year develops. A fund reads whether the system beneath a holding can replenish itself.
