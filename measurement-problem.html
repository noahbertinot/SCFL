The measurement problem

Conventional metrics report what a system produces. They do not report whether the relationships producing it still hold one another in place.

Sound parts, failing structure

A stone arch stays up only because each stone is held in place by the others. Every stone can be sound and the arch can still fall, because the failure lies in how the stones hold one another, not in any one stone.

A power grid shows the same thing in a working system. Every line can be within its limits while the network’s ability to absorb the loss of a line is shrinking. Output looks normal while the relationships that keep the system in place are coming loose.

What gets measured today

Performance is what a system produces. Stability is whether that output holds steady. Resilience, as usually measured, is whether the system persisted or recovered after a disturbance, which can only be known afterwards.

None of these is the condition of the relationships that produce the output. That condition is what the program calls coherence, and it is the quantity SCFL is designed to measure.

Why pairwise scores cannot see it

Most measures score relationships one pair at a time and add the results. Three relationships can each be acceptable and still fail to hold together as a set. The failure lives in the cycle, not in any single relation.

Not a contrived case

The example above is one case. To test whether it is typical, the program drew random signed graphs, held every pairwise statistic fixed, and varied only where the negative relations were placed.

Across 180 random graphs, the frustration index still varied in 840 of 900 conditions (93.3 percent). In a third of conditions, the same pairwise statistics produced both perfectly balanced and frustrated structures. Counting local triangles did not recover the global index either.

Limit. These are random graphs with uniform weights and arbitrary signs. They show that pairwise blindness is pervasive in principle. Whether it matters in measured systems is the next test, which has not yet been run.

Where you draw the box changes the answer

Any measurement needs a boundary: a feeder or a region, a department or a hospital. If the measured value changes with the box rather than with the system, it describes the researcher’s choice, not the system.

In a synthetic test with two planted clusters, the choice of box accounted for 66 percent of the variance in measured frustration under loose coupling and 77 percent under tight coupling. A common rule for deriving the box automatically recovered the clusters in 151 of 200 loosely coupled systems, but in only 15 of 200 tightly coupled ones, which is when boundaries matter most.

What this means. The boundary problem is a live risk, not a formality. It is listed as falsification condition F3, and it is not yet solved.

How coherence comes apart

Under load, relationships first deform while every part stays connected, then reorganize as some are lost and new dependencies form. Throughout, conventional outcome indicators may look normal.

Close to coherence, but not the same thing

Each of these may correlate with coherence, cause it or follow from it. None is assumed to be it.

IdeaHow it differs
PerformancePerformance is what a system produces, not the condition of the relationships that produce it.
StabilityOutput can be stable while the relationships beneath it are coming loose.
ResilienceResilience is observed persistence or recovery after a disturbance. Recoverability is the structural capacity measured beforehand.
OrderA structure can be highly regular while its relationships fail to hold one another in place.
EquilibriumAdaptive systems hold together while exchanging energy, material and information.
Quantum coherenceIt has a rigorous, domain-specific meaning. No identity with it is claimed.

What this page does not claim. It does not claim that coherence is a law of nature, that any single score constitutes it, that the boundary problem is solved, or that measured coherence necessarily predicts failure.
